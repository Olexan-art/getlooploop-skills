    Four readings decide whether a page is legible to a retrieval system. Here is what each one means when it is bad. Chunks that never reach the index A retrieval system reads a fixed number of passages from a page and ignores the rest, so anything below that cut is invisible no matter how good it is. Vectorscope applies the same cap the product applies and names the passages that fell outside it. If your answer, your differentiator or your FAQ sits below the cut, the fix is not more words, and semantic chunking will not rescue it either: a well-cut passage past the cap is still a passage nobody reads. Move that passage up, or split the page so it has a passage budget of its own. The extraction ratio The extraction ratio is how much of the page turned into readable text — a low one means most of what you shipped was navigation, boilerplate or markup that no model will ever see. A long page with a low ratio is a short page as far as retrieval is concerned. Extraction happens before any chunking, semantic chunking included, so text lost here is lost to every strategy. Common causes, in the order we see them: text rendered only by JavaScript, an article wrapped in so much chrome that the body is a minority of the document, and images carrying words that exist nowhere in the HTML. One enormous group usually means the page says one thing many times, which reads as thin to a retriever even when it reads as thorough to an editor. Several small groups with one passage each is the opposite problem: a page that changes subject constantly gives a retriever nothing complete to quote.     Show the full passageПоказати весь уривок
    Semantic chunking is splitting a page into passages where its meaning changes, instead of every fixed number of characters or at every heading. Vectorscope does not cut that way: it cuts at each h2 and then in fixed windows, and its semantic step comes after the cut, when the passages are grouped by meaning. 01 Fixed-length chunking Fixed-length chunking cuts every N characters and is the default in most pipelines because it is trivial to implement. It also cuts through the middle of a sentence, separates a heading from the answer under it, and produces passages that begin with half a word. Vectorscope uses it only inside a long section: a window of 2,000 characters with 200 characters of overlap, ending at a sentence break where one is close enough. 02 Heading-aware chunking Heading-aware chunking uses the page's own headings as the cut lines, so a passage arrives with the heading that names it. This is Vectorscope's first cut, and only an h2 makes one: an h3 stays inside the passage of the h2 above it, so a section of six h3 questions is one passage, or several windows of one. It also means your heading text is doing retrieval work. A section called "Overview" contributes nothing to the passage; a section called "Can I cancel at any time?" contributes the question itself. 03 Semantic chunking, and how it differs from grouping Semantic chunking compares neighbouring sentences and cuts where the meaning shifts, which gives passages that stand alone but needs an embedding pass over the page before a single chunk exists. Vectorscope does not do that step. Its semantic part is the grouping: every passage is embedded once it is cut, and passages that sit close in meaning are drawn as one group. So the map asks the question semantic chunking asks — where does one idea end? — of the passages your headings already made. A group spanning three h2 sections says the headings split one idea; a long section whose windows land in two groups says a single heading holds two.

On this page the meaning-based step is the grouping of passages that are already cut, not the cut itself. Chunk A passage of a page, cut so it can be read on its own. Retrieval systems store and return chunks, never whole pages, which is why a page's structure matters more than its length. Embedding A list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a passage, so that two passages about the same thing sit near each other. Comparing embeddings is how a retriever finds a passage without matching words. Cosine similarity A measure of how close two embeddings point in the same direction, from 1 for identical to 0 for unrelated. It is the number behind every claim that two passages are about the same thing. Retrieval-augmented generation Answering a question by first retrieving passages from a source and then writing from them, rather than from the model's memory alone. Every AI answer that cites a page did this, and the page it cited was a chunk. Extraction Turning a fetched HTML document into the readable text a model receives, discarding navigation, scripts and boilerplate. What extraction drops, no model ever sees.
